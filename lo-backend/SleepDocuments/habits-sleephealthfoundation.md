# Sleep Hygiene: Good Sleep Habits

Source: https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-topics/sleep-hygiene-good-sleep-habits

Good sleep habits, often called sleep hygiene, are simple everyday things that make good sleep more likely. Most adults need 7 to 9 hours.

In the evening, go to bed at around the same time each night. Your body clock works best with a regular routine and will start making you sleepy at bedtime. Don't push past that sleepy window by staying up, but don't go to bed too early either. Spend the last hour before bed doing something relaxing, like a warm bath, quiet reading or a warm milky drink, and go to the toilet before bed. Switch off screens 1 to 2 hours before bed and keep them out of the bedroom if you can.

Things to avoid in the evening:
- Caffeine close to bed, including coffee, tea, cola and soft drinks. Advice differs on the cut-off: some say 3 to 7 hours before bed, others say none after lunch if you sleep badly. Many people sleep better with less caffeine or only having it early in the day.
- Smoking before bed or during the night.
- Alcohol, which can help you fall asleep but makes it harder to stay asleep and makes snoring and sleep apnoea worse.
- Stimulating things in the last hour, like exercise, video games, TV and movies, serious conversations, social media, emails and texts.
- Bright lights and blue light from screens, which lower melatonin, the hormone that helps you sleep.
- Dozing on the couch in the evening, which uses up your sleep pressure and makes it harder to fall asleep in bed.

For meals, don't go to bed hungry or overly full. Finish dinner at least 2 hours before bed. A small snack at bedtime helps some people.

Make the bed comfortable and the room not too hot or cold. Remove distractions like TVs, radios, phones and laptops, and cover any clock so you aren't clock-watching. Keep kids and pets from disturbing you if you can.

During the day, keep out of bed. Using the bedroom to study, watch TV or make calls makes it harder to sleep there. Train your brain to link bed with sleep only. Exercise is good for sleep, ideally in the morning or before dinner, but not just before bed. Getting natural daylight, especially early in the day, helps your body clock and melatonin.

If you can't get to sleep, don't try to force it. If you're still awake after 20 to 30 minutes, get up and sit quietly in another dark room, without screens, food, drinks or chores, until you feel sleepy again. This keeps bed linked with sleep instead of frustration. Resting is still useful even if you don't sleep. Avoid calling yourself an insomniac, because that adds to the worry.

If your mind won't switch off, set aside a "worry time" in the evening to go over the day, plan and think of solutions, then leave those thoughts until tomorrow. Use the hour before bed to wind down with quiet music or relaxation. You can't stop thinking completely, but you can steer towards calmer thoughts, like imagining a favourite holiday spot. If you still can't sleep, leave the bedroom and come back when you're sleepy.

Short naps of around 30 minutes are usually fine if they don't cause problems. Evening naps or dozing in front of the TV make it harder to sleep at night.

Some medicines help sleep and others keep you awake, so only take them as a doctor or pharmacist advises. Sleeping pills are only meant for short-term or occasional use under a doctor's care.

Poor sleepers shouldn't spend too long in bed, about 8 hours at most. Lying in bed longer teaches your body that drifting in and out of sleep is normal. Going to bed a bit later can be one of the best ways to cut down time lying awake.

Stick to your routine on most days. Sleep won't improve overnight, but it will get better if you keep the good habits going. Find what works for you. If nothing helps, see your GP, who may suggest these strategies or refer you to a sleep specialist.
