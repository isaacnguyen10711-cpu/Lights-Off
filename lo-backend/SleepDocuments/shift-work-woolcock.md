# How Shift Work Affects Your Sleep

Source: https://www.woolcock.org.au/news/please-explain-how-shift-work-affects-your-sleep

Humans are built to be awake in daylight and asleep in the dark. Your body clock runs your circadian rhythms, which make you alert in the day and drowsy as it gets dark. Working at night goes directly against this, so night shift feels a lot like jet lag.

Two things make night shifts hard. First, the body clock makes you feel worst in the early hours of the morning, when you'd normally be asleep. Second, "sleep pressure" builds the longer you stay awake, pushing you towards sleep. Light matters too: bright morning light wakes you up, which is why it's hard to fall asleep after getting home from a night shift.

After about three or four night shifts in a row, the body clock shifts enough to cope a bit better. Around 20 percent of Australian workers do shift work, mostly in healthcare, hospitality, transport, manufacturing, mining, media and food production. Rotating rosters are now more common than permanent nights. When the gap between different shifts is too short, the body clock can't adjust in time.

Shift work sleep disorder involves poor sleep, trouble concentrating, headaches, low energy and tiredness. Some people handle shift work much better than others, and the same coping tricks don't work for everyone.

Tips for coping with night shift:
- Treat your post-shift sleep like a normal night's sleep with a regular routine: dim the lights, avoid screens, skip heavy meals and do something calming like reading or meditating.
- Get bright light while working to stay alert, but block morning light on the way home, for example with dark sunglasses. Make the bedroom as dark as possible, and use an eye mask if your curtains let light through.
- Keep the bedroom cool and quiet. Earplugs, noise-cancelling devices or calming sounds can help.
- Before your first night shift, either get up early and sleep again in the afternoon, or take a short power nap. Keep naps under about 20 minutes, because longer naps drop you into deep sleep and leave you groggy.
- After your last night shift, get back to a normal routine quickly. A short sleep and then staying up until night is better than staying awake for 24 hours straight.
- Avoid alcohol, caffeine, other stimulants and heavy protein-rich meals before sleeping, whether you sleep in the day or at night. Hard exercise just before bed can keep you awake, so work out before your shift instead of after.
- Sleeping pills are only meant for short-term use, and there isn't good evidence that melatonin or over-the-counter sleep aids help. See a doctor before taking anything for sleep.
