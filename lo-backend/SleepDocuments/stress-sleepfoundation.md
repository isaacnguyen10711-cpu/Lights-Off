# Stress and Insomnia

Source: https://www.sleepfoundation.org/insomnia/stress-and-insomnia

Stress and sleep feed into each other. Stress makes it harder to fall asleep and stay asleep, and poor sleep makes stress harder to handle the next day. Sleep often improves once a stressful period ends, but some people get stuck in a loop where bad nights cause daytime anxiety, which causes more bad nights. Working on either the stress or the sleep usually helps the other.

Insomnia means ongoing trouble falling asleep, staying asleep or getting good-quality sleep, even when you have enough time and a comfortable place to sleep. It leads to daytime tiredness, fatigue and irritability. Between a third and two thirds of adults have bouts of it at some point. Ongoing stress also changes how you sleep, cutting down deep sleep and disturbing REM (dreaming) sleep.

Short-term insomnia lasts under three months and is usually triggered by a sudden stress, like relationship problems, work issues, money trouble, grief, a health scare, or a change in where you sleep (moving house, travelling, sharing a room with a new baby or a sibling).

Chronic insomnia means trouble sleeping at least three nights a week for three months or more. Ongoing stresses like an unhappy job, family conflict, divorce, losing someone, serious illness, big life changes or money worries can drive it. Changes to your sleep schedule caused by life events can trigger it too. People with anxiety are more at risk. Once it sets in, many people start worrying about sleep itself, which adds stress and makes the insomnia worse.

When you're stressed, your body releases stress hormones like cortisol and adrenaline. Cortisol is normally highest just after waking and drops through the day. Stress hormones make you feel wired and alert, and you often crash afterwards. Short bursts of stress are mostly harmless, but long-term stress keeps heart rate and blood pressure up and affects digestion, muscles (tension, headaches, back pain), breathing, the nervous system and reproductive health.

Ways to manage stress for better sleep:
- Eat a balanced diet, exercise during the week, and use slow breathing or other relaxation techniques.
- Keep a healthy balance between work and life, and try not to bottle stress up.
- Cognitive behavioural stress management, a short type of talk therapy, helps people spot negative thoughts and replace them with more helpful ones.
- Cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) works well for insomnia. It challenges unhelpful beliefs about sleep and teaches sleep restriction, getting out of bed on sleepless nights, good sleep habits and relaxation skills.
- See a doctor straight away if you have thoughts of suicide, are misusing drugs or alcohol, or feel you can't cope with daily life.

Sleep habits that help when you're stressed:
- Go to bed and get up at the same times every day, including weekends and when travelling.
- Keep the bedroom dim, quiet and cool, around 18 degrees Celsius.
- Keep TVs, computers and phones out of the bedroom, because their light can get in the way of sleep.
- Avoid caffeine and nicotine in the hours before bed.
- Skip alcohol before bed. It makes you drowsy at first but breaks up your sleep later in the night.
- Exercise moderately in the morning or early afternoon.
- If you can't fall asleep within 15 to 20 minutes, get up and do something calm somewhere else, like reading, meditating or listening to quiet music. Don't check your phone or the clock. If the problem keeps going, see a doctor.
