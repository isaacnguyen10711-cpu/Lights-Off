# How Electronics Affect Sleep

Source: https://www.sleepfoundation.org/how-sleep-works/how-electronics-affect-sleep

Using phones, computers and tablets before bed can make it harder to fall asleep and lower sleep quality. There are two reasons. The blue light from screens holds back melatonin, the hormone that makes you sleepy in the evening. And content like social media, games or work emails keeps your brain switched on when it should be winding down. Together they push your body clock later.

Your body clock runs on a roughly 24-hour cycle. In the morning your body releases cortisol, which makes you alert. As it gets dark, it releases melatonin, which makes you sleepy. Phones, tablets, computers, and LED and fluorescent lights all give off blue light, which delays or reduces melatonin and makes you feel less sleepy. Blue light can also cut down on deep sleep and REM (dreaming) sleep, both of which matter for thinking and memory.

Bright room lighting has a similar effect. One study found that bright bedroom light delayed melatonin by up to 90 minutes compared with dim light. Normal indoor light of around 100 lux or more can be enough to hold melatonin back. Red, orange and yellow light barely affect the body clock, so dim warm light is best for reading at night. E-readers give off less blue light than phones, but turn the brightness down anyway.

Young people are especially affected. Screen use before bed is linked to taking longer to fall asleep, getting less good-quality sleep and feeling tired the next day. Heavy screen use during the day is also linked to shorter sleep in teenagers.

Tips if you need screens at night, for example for work or study:
- Cut down on screen time overall, not just at night.
- Keep a regular bedtime and spend the last hour before bed on relaxing things that don't involve screens.
- Make the bedroom a screen-free zone, including the TV.
- Keep bedroom lights dim in the evening.
- Turn on night mode, which cuts blue light, and turn the brightness down by hand if it doesn't do that automatically.
- Blue-light-blocking glasses with orange lenses are fairly cheap and have worked well in some studies.

Night mode and blue-light glasses help, but using devices less before bed works better.
