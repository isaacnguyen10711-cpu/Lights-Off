# Exercise and Sleep
Source: https://www.sleepfoundation.org/physical-activity/exercise-and-sleep

(Summary written in our own words, not the original article text.)

Exercise and sleep affect each other in both directions. Regular exercise helps many people fall asleep faster, spend less time awake during the night and sleep better overall. Poor sleep, in turn, tends to make people less active the next day.

Moderate to vigorous exercise is linked to falling asleep sooner, waking less in the night, less daytime sleepiness and, for some people, less need for sleeping pills. Exercise also helps sleep indirectly. It helps control weight, which lowers the risk of obstructive sleep apnoea, and regular aerobic exercise has eased sleep apnoea symptoms even without weight loss. One study of university students found that exercise lowered stress during exam periods.

Physical work isn't the same as exercise. Manual labour jobs often cause aches and pains, and long hours of physical work add stress and fatigue, both of which can make sleep worse.

Old sleep advice says to avoid intense exercise in the 3 hours before bed, because it raises heart rate, body temperature and adrenaline. Newer research is more mixed. Many people who exercise in the evening, even after 8pm, still fall asleep quickly, get plenty of deep sleep and wake up refreshed. One study found that evening exercise led to more deep sleep. The main thing to watch is very hard workouts in the last hour before bed. These keep body temperature high, which is linked to lying awake longer and less total sleep.

The best time to exercise depends on your schedule, how hard you work out and how your body reacts. Gentle options like yoga, stretching, tai chi and breathing exercises are fine close to bed and may even help.

Sleep problems make people less likely to exercise. Adults with insomnia or sleep apnoea are usually less active. In one study, every extra 30 minutes it took to fall asleep meant about a minute less exercise the next day. Morning people also tend to be more active than night owls. Good sleep makes you feel more rested and motivated to exercise, but it won't change your activity levels on its own.

Common questions:

- Stretching before bed is generally fine. Moderate activity near bedtime has little evidence of harming sleep.
- Sleeping after exercise doesn't cause weight gain. Not getting enough sleep is the bigger problem, because it raises hunger hormones and lowers the hormone that makes you feel full.
- Exercise can help with insomnia, but very hard exercise finishing within an hour of bed may disrupt sleep. Cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is still the main recommended treatment.
