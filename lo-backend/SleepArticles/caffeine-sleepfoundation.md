# Caffeine and Sleep
Source: https://www.sleepfoundation.org/nutrition/caffeine-and-sleep

(Summary written in our own words, not the original article text.)

Most adults have caffeine every day, from coffee, tea, chocolate, soft drinks, energy drinks and some medicines. It helps with alertness and mood, but it can cause jitters, headaches and nervousness, and it can hurt sleep, especially when you have it later in the day. Sleep Foundation data from about 160,000 people found that roughly 88% of those who regularly have caffeine in the afternoon report at least one sleep problem.

Caffeine works by blocking adenosine. Adenosine is a chemical that builds up in the brain the longer you're awake and makes you feel sleepy. Blocking it keeps you alert, but it also means caffeine can push your bedtime later, shorten your sleep, make sleep feel less satisfying and cut down on deep sleep, the stage that leaves you feeling refreshed.

Lots of caffeine, or caffeine close to bed, can cause insomnia. People who sleep badly often reach for more caffeine to get through the day, which makes the next night worse. Occasional caffeine users can be hit harder than daily users. People with ongoing insomnia may sleep better if they cut back, stop completely, or only have caffeine early in the day.

Caffeine starts working after about 30 minutes, and the effects can last five hours or more. Its half-life (how long the body takes to clear half of it) ranges from about 2 to 12 hours depending on the person. Dose, how often you have it and your genes all play a part. Smokers clear caffeine faster, and people late in pregnancy clear it more slowly. Even if you can fall asleep after caffeine, your sleep may be lighter and lower quality than it would have been.

Some people feel tired even with caffeine. This can be a vicious cycle, where caffeine hurts sleep, poor sleep causes tiredness, and you have more caffeine to cope. Tolerance can also blunt its effects over time, and morning sleepiness in regular users can be a withdrawal symptom.

Up to 400 mg a day is generally considered safe for adults, though people react differently. Rough amounts: a cup of coffee has about 100 to 200 mg, an energy drink 50 to 300 mg, a can of soft drink 0 to 70 mg, and a cup of tea 40 to 120 mg.

The usual advice is to stop caffeine at least 8 hours before bed. For a 10pm bedtime, that means none after 2pm. If you're sleeping badly, track when you have caffeine and how you sleep that night. Some people do better with 10 hours or more without it before bed.

Trouble falling or staying asleep, nausea, headaches or nervousness can be signs of too much caffeine. If you're very sleepy during the day and caffeine isn't helping, talk to a doctor, because it could be a sleep disorder. If you cut back, do it gradually to avoid withdrawal headaches, anxiety and low mood.

Other ways to stay alert without caffeine:

- Get bright light in the morning, ideally natural light outside.
- Keep a regular sleep schedule and a relaxing bedroom.
- Exercise, which boosts alertness and can improve sleep.
- Take a short power nap of up to 20 minutes. It doesn't replace night-time sleep, though.
